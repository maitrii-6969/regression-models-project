Motor Trend: Is Automatic or Manual Transmission Better for MPG?

Maitreya Tarmale

October 27, 2026

Executive Summary

Motor Trend asked two questions: (1) is an automatic or manual transmission better for MPG, and (2) how large is the MPG difference? Using the mtcars data (32 cars), a simple comparison says manual cars get about 7.2 MPG more than automatics. That number is misleading, because manual cars in this data are also lighter. After adjusting for weight and quarter-mile time, manual transmission is associated with about 2.9 MPG more (95% CI: 0.05 to 5.83 MPG, p = 0.047). So manual looks better for MPG, but the evidence is weak and the true advantage could be close to zero. Weight is by far the stronger predictor of fuel economy.

Manual cars have a higher mean MPG (24.4 vs 17.1) but are also much lighter (2.4 vs 3.8 thousand lb). Weight is strongly correlated with MPG (r = -0.87), so transmission and weight are confounded (Figure 1 and Figure 2 in the Appendix).

Model Selection Strategy

I fit nested models, always keeping am as the variable of interest, and compared them with ANOVA (F-tests) and adjusted R-squared.

fit1 <- lm(mpg ~ am, data = mtcars)                  # unadjusted
fit2 <- lm(mpg ~ am + wt, data = mtcars)             # + weight
fit3 <- lm(mpg ~ am + wt + qsec, data = mtcars)      # + quarter-mile time
fit4 <- lm(mpg ~ am * wt + qsec, data = mtcars)      # + am:wt interaction
fitAll <- lm(mpg ~ ., data = mtcars)                 # all variables
anova(fit1, fit2, fit3)

##  fit1  fit2  fit3  fit4   all 
## 0.338 0.736 0.834 0.880 0.807
  • fit1 (am only) explains only 36% of the variance.
  • Adding wt raises adjusted R-squared to 0.74, and adding qsec raises it to 0.83. Each addition is significant by the F-test.
  • Adding the am:wt interaction (fit4) is also significant (F-test p = 0.002) and raises adjusted R-squared to 0.88. It means the benefit of a manual transmission depends on the car’s weight. I keep the simpler fit3 for the headline answer because a single number is easier to interpret and report, but the interaction is a real feature of the data, so I discuss it as a limitation below.
  • The full model with every variable has a lower adjusted R-squared than fit3 and no significant terms, which is a sign of overfitting and collinearity (wt, disp, hp are strongly correlated).

##             2.5 % 97.5 %
## (Intercept) -4.64  23.87
## amManual     0.05   5.83
## wt          -5.37  -2.46
## qsec         0.63   1.82

Holding weight and quarter-mile time constant:

  • am (Manual): manual cars get about 2.94 MPG more than automatics (95% CI 0.05 to 5.83).
  • wt: each additional 1,000 lb lowers MPG by about 3.92 (95% CI -5.37 to -2.46).
  • qsec: each additional second of quarter-mile time is associated with about 1.23 MPG more (95% CI 0.63 to 1.82).
  • Intercept (9.6) is the expected MPG for an automatic car with wt = 0 and qsec = 0, which is outside the data, so it has no practical meaning.

The model explains about 85% of the variation in MPG (R-squared = 0.85, adjusted 0.83).

The residuals vs fitted plot shows no strong pattern, and the Q-Q plot is close to a straight line. The Shapiro-Wilk test does not reject normality (p = 0.08). No point is highly influential: the largest Cook’s distance is about 0.35 (Chrysler Imperial), well below 1. The model assumptions look reasonable.

Uncertainty and Inference

t.test(mpg ~ am, data = mtcars)
## 
##  Welch Two Sample t-test
## 
## data:  mpg by am
## t = -3.7671, df = 18.332, p-value = 0.001374
## alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group Automatic and group Manual is not equal to 0
## 95 percent confidence interval:
##  -11.280194  -3.209684
## sample estimates:
## mean in group Automatic    mean in group Manual 
##                17.14737                24.39231

The unadjusted t-test shows a clear difference (about 7.2 MPG, p = 0.001), but that difference is mostly explained by weight. In the adjusted model, the am effect has p = 0.047 and a confidence interval that nearly reaches zero. So we can say manual is associated with higher MPG, but we cannot be confident about how much. With only 32 cars (13 manual) and observational data, this is not a causal result.

Conclusion

  1. Is manual better for MPG? Yes in this data, but only modestly once weight and qsec are accounted for.
  2. How big is the difference? About 2.9 MPG (95% CI 0.05 to 5.8), against 7.2 MPG without adjustment.

Limitations: small sample, observational data, cars are not randomly assigned to a transmission type, and the significant weight-by-transmission interaction means the single “2.9 MPG” figure is an average; the manual advantage differs between light and heavy cars (see summary(fit4)).
